## Getting an account

You do not sign yourself up. Somebody who runs the installation creates your account, or
opens registration deliberately and tells you. If you have been given an address and nothing
else, ask them.

Once you have an account you sign in with your **email address** and password. Your username
is how you are *listed*, not how you sign in — changing it later changes nothing about
signing in.

Some installations also offer **Google, GitHub or another external provider**. If yours does,
the buttons are on the sign-in page. An external login federates into a normal local account;
it does not create a second kind of user.

If two-factor authentication is switched on for your account, you are asked for a code after
your password — from an authenticator app, or emailed, or texted. See
[Your account and settings](features/account-and-settings.md).

## What you see first

By default the application opens on the **map**. You can change that to the **dashboard** in
Settings → Accessibility → *Open the app on*; the choice stays on the device you set it on,
because a phone and a workstation reasonably want different answers.

The screen has three parts:

1. **The top bar** — search, the notification bell, your account menu, the language switch.
2. **The navigation rail** down the left — grouped destinations (below).
3. **The work area** — whatever page you are on.

## The navigation rail

The rail shows only what your rights let you reach, so yours may be shorter than a
colleague's. Nothing is hidden to be coy — a group with no pages you may open is not drawn
at all, rather than opening onto nothing.

## Your first hour: a suggested order

1. **Set your language.** Top bar. English and Romanian ship with the application, and the
   choice follows your account — so the emails it sends you arrive in it too, not just the
   screens.
2. **Fill in your profile.** Settings → Profile. A **display name** in particular: without
   one you appear beside everything you add under a generated name. Each field carries its
   own small audience icon — only you, your caving groups, or everybody signed in.
3. **Check your notifications.** Settings → Notifications is a grid: kinds of event down the
   side, ways of reaching you across the top. It is worth five minutes now rather than
   twenty emails later. See [Notifications](features/notifications.md).
4. **Open the map and look around.** Turn layers on and off. Click something. Get a feel for
   the layer tree and the details panel.
5. **Find one cave you know well** and read its page top to bottom. That page is the densest
   thing in the application and everything else makes more sense afterwards.

## Two habits worth forming early

**Read the small print on the screen.** This application says things out loud that most
software leaves you to guess: *"Counted over the trips you may read"*, *"Approximate
location — exact coordinates are protected"*, *"This is a draft. Nobody has been notified"*.
Those lines are not decoration. They are usually the answer to the question you were about
to ask.

**Assume other people see something different.** Two members can look at the same cave, the
same trip, the same total, and honestly see different numbers, because what each of them may
read differs. This is not a bug and the application will usually tell you when it is
happening.

## Getting around quickly

- **Search** in the top bar covers caves, features, trips, camps, saved views, places
  (via an external geocoder) *and the text inside documents*. See
  [Search and filters](features/search-and-filters.md).
- **Right-click the map** for a context menu: add a feature, new cave here, new entrance
  here, copy coordinates.
- **Pop a panel out** into its own window if you have two monitors — the registry, the
  selection panel, the 3D scene and the survey viewer all do this, and they stay in sync.
  See [Saved views, panels and multi-window](features/saved-views-and-windows.md).
